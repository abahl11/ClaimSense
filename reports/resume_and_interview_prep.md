## Likely interview questions

**1. Why a time-based split instead of a random one?**

Because the model will always predict future claims. A random split would put claims from the test period into training. Costs grew 3.7x across the data, and the test period's median cost is 7,640 against 2,819 in training, so a random split would give flattering results that would not hold in production. Model selection used 5 expanding-window folds inside the training period, and the test set was scored once.

**2. How did you handle the skewed target?**

I trained on log cost, which makes errors relative so a few huge claims do not dominate, and selected models by RMSE on the log scale. Exponentiating a log prediction gives roughly the median, so for reserves I applied Duan's smearing factor (1.434), estimated from out-of-fold residuals so that boosting's small in-sample residuals do not understate it. I also tried Tweedie and Gamma losses on the raw cost.

**3. Costs rise over time. How can a model trained on old claims be trusted?**

I compared four approaches with cross-validation: log cost, the ratio to the adjuster's estimate (which is recorded at current prices), and trend-adjusted versions of both. Cross-validation chose **LightGBM, A: log cost**; the test set agreed. In production I would monitor predicted vs actual by accident quarter, recalibrate as claims close and retrain at least yearly.

**4. What did the NLP add?**

In an ablation on the same model, the text features reduced cross-validated RMSLE by 21%. The keyword taxonomy gives interpretable labels (body part, nature, cause; 87% of descriptions get a nature label); TF-IDF with SVD captures wording beyond the keywords. The sentence-embedding clusters did not improve cross-validation by more than the pre-set tolerance, so the final model leaves them out: a result, not a failure.

**5. How did you avoid leakage?**

Every learned step (imputation medians, scaling, one-hot levels, TF-IDF, SVD, KMeans) is inside one scikit-learn pipeline fit on training rows only, and refit in each fold. Cleaning rules use fixed thresholds. The high-cost threshold comes from the training target only. Tests check that the target is never an input and that the fitted steps only saw training rows.

**6. How do you choose the classifier threshold?**

From review capacity: the probability at which 10% of the latest training claims would be flagged (0.803). On the test set that flags 21.1% of claims, because more claims really are expensive after the price rise; a team with fixed capacity would simply review the top-ranked claims.

**7. Why is recall at 10% capacity only 32.2%?**

Because 31.0% of test claims are high-cost against 10.0% in training. Reviewing 10% of claims can catch at most 32.2% of them. The precision at that capacity is 100.0%, and with a label defined within each accident year reviewing the top 10% catches 55.3% of claims that are unusually severe for their year (vs 54.8% ranking by the initial estimate). The gain is marginal: the adjuster's initial estimate already carries most of the signal for ranking claims by severity. The model's value is elsewhere: accurate cost levels and totals, an uncertainty range, and drivers.

**8. What drives the predictions?**

By SHAP (one-hot columns summed per concept), the top drivers are Initial estimate, Description wording (TF-IDF/SVD), Initial estimate / weekly wage, Weekly wage. SHAP shows what the model relies on, not causes, and each prediction in the app comes with its own drivers.

**9. Did you use gender, and is the model fair?**

I compared the model with and without gender: removing it changes cross-validated RMSLE by -0.0004. That is negligible, so the recommendation is to exclude it. I also compare error and flag rates across genders and age bands on the test set and would re-run those checks at every retrain.

**10. What are the main limitations?**

The data is synthetic; the top 1% of claims are under-predicted (the model predicts 0.23 of their actual cost) because what makes them huge happens after reporting; and costs drift, so the model and its calibration need regular refreshing. Interval coverage on the test set is 80.9% against a nominal 80%. Tuning with Optuna (25 trials) moved cross-validated RMSLE from 0.5768 to 0.5702.
